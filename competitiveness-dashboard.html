Total vs Per Capita — Why It Matters: MA's 5.3% total PCE growth led all 50 states, but MA also gained ~66K international immigrants in 2024, growing its consumer base. On a per capita basis, MA's PCE growth drops to 4.2% and NH's is 3.9% — a gap of just 0.3pp instead of 0.9pp. For per capita personal income growth, NH actually leads MA: +3.5% vs +3.3%. Population (MA ~7.0M vs NH ~1.4M) inflates total figures.
The Right-to-Shelter Factor: MA spent ~$932M in FY2024 and $1B+ projected in FY2025 on emergency shelters under its 1986 right-to-shelter law, driven by a migrant influx (~half of 7,500 capped families are migrants/asylum seekers). That government shelter spending does not directly count as PCE — BEA classifies it as government consumption expenditure. But the 66K+ new residents do spend money (rent, food, goods), inflating total PCE mechanically. Cash transfers (EA grants, SNAP/EBT) that flow to households and get spent do appear in PCE. Per capita figures strip out the population growth effect. NH has no comparable shelter mandate or influx.

⚠ Behind the Rankings

The Proficiency Problem

MA ranks #1 on NAEP — but that means being the best in a country where most students can't meet proficiency standards. On MA's own MCAS test, fewer than half of students meet grade-level expectations. The pandemic made it worse, and the state hasn't recovered.

Demography Is (Almost) Destiny

The UMass Donahue Institute found that 84% of variation in MCAS scores across MA districts is explained by socioeconomic factors — real estate values, poverty rates, demographic composition. The test measures zip codes as much as schools.

Districts like Weston (median home ~$1.5M) and Wellesley routinely see 70–80%+ proficiency. Gateway Cities like Lawrence, Holyoke, Springfield, and Brockton — where child poverty exceeds 20% — often fall below 20–25%.

In November 2024, MA voters passed Question 2, removing MCAS as a graduation requirement. DESE reports the 2025 results already show more students leaving answers blank or responding off-topic — suggesting reduced stakes are affecting effort.

NH avoids some of this dynamic through lower inequality and smaller urban centers, but also lacks the density of elite institutions that inflate MA's statewide averages.

UMass Donahue Institute · MA DESE · WBUR · Boston Herald · Boston25
Bottom line: MA is #1 in NAEP, #1 in bachelor's attainment, top-5 in spending — and still, 6 in 10 students don't meet grade-level expectations on the state's own test. NH outperforms national averages on NAEP with the 2nd-lowest student-teacher ratio and less spending inequality across districts. Both states are "best in the nation" — in a nation where the bar is extremely low. The real story is the gap between wealthy suburbs and working-class cities within each state.
